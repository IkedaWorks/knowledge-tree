# Analytical Derivation of Capacitance in Canonical Geometries

## Problem Statement and Conditions

Calculating the capacitance $C$ of an arbitrary two-conductor system requires determining the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ generated by separated charges $+Q$ and $-Q$, integrating this field to find the potential difference $V$, and evaluating the geometric ratio $C = Q / V$.

This protocol applies to highly symmetric geometries where Gauss's Law easily yields the field $\mathbf{E}$. The boundary conditions assume ideal conductors in a vacuum ($\varepsilon_0$) with charges uniformly distributed across their surfaces.

## Algorithmic Step-by-Step Procedure

To derive the capacitance of any symmetrical two-conductor geometry, execute the following three-step protocol:

1. **Calculate the Electric Field ($\mathbf{E}$):** Define a suitable Gaussian surface passing through the region between the conductors and evaluate Gauss's Law:
   $$\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

2. **Compute the Potential Difference ($V$):** Integrate the electric field along a path from the positive conductor to the negative conductor:
   $$V = -\int_{\text{negative}}^{\text{positive}} \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_{a}^{b} E \, dr$$

3. **Evaluate Capacitance ($C$):** Substitute the derived potential $V$ into the fundamental relation:
   $$C = \frac{Q}{V}$$

## Complete Solved Application

### 1. Parallel-Plate Geometry

Consider two flat conducting plates of surface area $A$ separated by a small distance $d$, carrying charges $+Q$ and $-Q$.

![Cylindrical Gaussian surface intersecting a parallel-plate conductor to evaluate electric flux](./../../../../gaussian-surface-parallel.svg)
*Figure 1: Cross-sectional representation of a parallel-plate capacitor showing the cylindrical Gaussian surface used to derive the uniform internal electric field.*

#### Execution:
1. **Electric Field via Gauss:** Construct a cylindrical Gaussian surface intersecting the positive plate. The electric flux passes exclusively through the inner end-face of area $A$:
   $$E \cdot A = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}$$

2. **Potential Difference:** Integrate $E$ across the uniform separation gap $d$:
   $$V = \int_{0}^{d} E \, dx = \left(\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}\right) d$$

3. **Capacitance:**
   $$C = \frac{Q}{\left(\frac{Q d}{\varepsilon_0 A}\right)} \implies C = \varepsilon_0 \frac{A}{d}$$

---

### 2. Cylindrical Geometry (Coaxial Cable)

Consider an inner conducting cylinder of radius $a$ and an outer concentric cylindrical shell of radius $b$, both of length $L$ ($L \gg b$), carrying charges $+Q$ and $-Q$.

![Isometric 3D model of a coaxial cylindrical capacitor with length L](./../../../../capacitance-cylindrical-3d.svg)
*Figure 2A: 3D isometric perspective of a coaxial cylindrical capacitor highlighting radial field lines and symmetry axes.*

![Cross-sectional view of concentric cylindrical conductors with a radial Gaussian surface.](./../../../../capacitance-cylindrical-crosssection.svg)
*Figure 2B: 2D cross-section showing the Gaussian surface of radius $r$ situated strictly between inner radius $a$ and outer radius $b$.*

#### Execution:
1. **Electric Field via Gauss:** Choose a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ ($a < r < b$) and length $L$:
   $$\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = E \cdot (2\pi r L) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{Q}{2\pi \varepsilon_0 L r}$$

2. **Potential Difference:** Integrate radially from $a$ to $b$:
   $$V = \int_{a}^{b} \frac{Q}{2\pi \varepsilon_0 L r} \, dr = \frac{Q}{2\pi \varepsilon_0 L} \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$$

3. **Capacitance:**
   $$C = \frac{Q}{\frac{Q}{2\pi \varepsilon_0 L} \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)} \implies C = \frac{2\pi \varepsilon_0 L}{\ln(b/a)}$$

---

### 3. Spherical Geometry

Consider an inner solid conducting sphere of radius $a$ surrounded by a concentric spherical conducting shell of inner radius $b$, carrying charges $+Q$ and $-Q$.

![Concentric spherical capacitor cross-section illustrating radii a, b, and the Gaussian radius r.](./../../../../capacitance-spherical-derivation.svg)
*Figure 3: Geometric setup for a spherical capacitor displaying the inner sphere of radius $a$, outer shell of radius $b$, and concentric Gaussian surface at $r$.*

#### Execution:
1. **Electric Field via Gauss:** Choose a concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ ($a < r < b$):
   $$\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = E \cdot (4\pi r^2) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r^2}$$

2. **Potential Difference:** Integrate radially from $a$ to $b$:
   $$V = \int_{a}^{b} \frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r^2} \, dr = \frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b} \right) = \frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{b - a}{a b} \right)$$

3. **Capacitance:**
   $$C = \frac{Q}{\frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{b - a}{a b} \right)} \implies C = 4\pi \varepsilon_0 \left( \frac{a b}{b - a} \right)$$

## Common Pitfalls and Boundary Cases

- **Path Integration Signs:** Swapping the limits of integration can introduce an unphysical negative sign for $V$. Capacitance $C$ is strictly a positive scalar quantity; always evaluate $V$ as a positive magnitude ($V = |\Delta V|$).
- **Infinite Approximation Failure:** The parallel-plate formula $C = \varepsilon_0 A / d$ assumes $d \ll \sqrt{A}$. When $d \approx \sqrt{A}$, edge effects dominate and $C$ exceeds the calculated geometric value.
- **Single Isolated Conductor:** For an isolated conductor (e.g., an isolated sphere of radius $R$), the outer conductor is assumed to reside at infinity ($b \to \infty$). Applying this limit to the spherical model yields $C = 4\pi \varepsilon_0 R$.
