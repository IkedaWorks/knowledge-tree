

# 📐 Theory: Linear Distributions — Generic Derivation of a Charged Wire

The calculation of the electric field for macroscopic bodies consists of transforming discrete Coulomb's Law into a continuous limit operation. Understanding the mechanics of this derivation eliminates the need to memorize complex formulas and builds the geometric foundation necessary for upper-level electromagnetism.

---

## 🌎 Analysis Scenario and Axis Selection

Imagine a straight conducting wire, with negligible thickness and volume, positioned exactly along the **z-axis**. Our goal is to map the electric field generated by this body at any point $P$ located in space.



> 💡 **Engineering Insight:** Why do we lock the problem into a specific axis? We choose the z-axis to create artificial symmetry. If the wire were randomly thrown across the diagonal of a cube, the problem would require working with simultaneous three-dimensional differential equations in Linear Algebra. By aligning the wire to the z-axis and choosing point $P$ perpendicular to it, we radically simplify nature's native trigonometry.

---

## 🛠️ The Resolution Pipeline (4-Step Algorithm)

### Step 1: Transitioning from the Point-Charge to the Infinitesimal Model (The Physics)

The classic Coulomb equation for discrete point charges is based on the summation of field vectors:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \sum_{i} \frac{q_i}{|\vec{r}_{\text{rel}}|^3} \vec{r}_{\text{rel}}
$$

For macroscopic bodies, the scenario changes. We cannot use the average variation operator ($\Delta$) because it would only compute an average, homogeneous distribution (high school scope). In the real engineering world, charge can be more concentrated at one end than the other.

To solve this, we use **differential calculus**: we divide the wire into infinite pieces of length so small that they approach zero ($dl$). Each microscopic piece will contain an infinitesimal amount of charge ($dq$).

Since we cannot integrate (sum) with respect to the "charge" variable ($dq$), we need to translate this physics into geometry. This is where **Linear Charge Density ($\lambda$)** comes in:

$$
\lambda = \frac{\text{Total Charge}}{\text{Total Length}} = \frac{Q}{L} \quad \left[\text{Unit: } \frac{\text{Coulomb}}{\text{meter}}\right]
$$

If the charge is continuously distributed, the ratio holds true at the microscopic scale:

$$
\lambda = \frac{dq}{dl} \implies dq = \lambda \cdot dl
$$

Since our wire is strictly restricted along the z-axis, the infinitesimal length element $dl$ is nothing more than a tiny variation in height $z$, meaning $dl = dz'$. Thus, our charge translation becomes:

$$
dq = \lambda \, dz'
$$

Substituting this identity into the infinitesimal Coulomb's Law and applying the integral (which, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, undoes the effect of the field derivative $\int d\vec{E} = \vec{E}$), we obtain our setup equation:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int \frac{\lambda dz'}{|\vec{r}_{\text{rel}}|^3} \vec{r}_{\text{rel}}
$$

### Step 2: The Conversion Kit (Cylindrical Translation and the Trigonometric Circle)

If we try to solve the relative distance vector $|\vec{r}_{\text{rel}}|$ using conventional Cartesian coordinates ($x, y, z$), we will fall into heavy three-dimensional polynomial equations (the equivalent of calculating the spatial diagonal of a prism for every single piece of wire). To bypass this, we perform a coordinate transition.

#### Where does this math come from? (The Cartesian $\to$ Polar $\to$ Cylindrical Leap)

You are probably already familiar with the classic **Trigonometric Circle**, where the radius is always equal to 1. In it, any point on the edge of the circle is mapped by projecting the shadow of the radius onto the horizontal and vertical axes: the x-axis becomes $\cos(\phi)$ and the y-axis becomes $\sin(\phi)$.

**Polar Coordinates** expand this concept for engineering. Instead of locking the radius at 1, we allow the circle to grow to any size (a variable radius that we will call $\rho$). Now, to find any point on a two-dimensional plane ($xy$), you only need to know the straight-line distance to the center ($\rho$) and the angle of inclination $\phi$ relative to the horizon:

$$
x = \rho \cos(\phi)
$$

$$
y = \rho \sin(\phi)
$$

**Cylindrical Coordinates** are the final evolution of this system into three-dimensional space: they take the circular base of polar coordinates and add a vertical height axis ($z$). Imagine a vertical flagpole driven right into the center of the polar table. This flagpole is the z-axis. A circle that gains height geometrically transforms into a **Cilinder**.



> ✈️ **The Fighter Radar Analogy:** Imagine you are piloting a military fighter jet. If your radar display showed an incoming enemy missile in Cartesian coordinates like $(3, 4, 5)$, you would die trying to mentally calculate the three-dimensional diagonal of that vector.
>
> That is why radar screens use the cylindrical/polar system. The panel consists of concentric circles: the direct distance to the target is the radius ($\rho$), the direction on the horizon (360°) is given by the angle ($\phi$), and the altitude difference is the z-axis (height). You glance at it and locate the threat instantly using just one axis and one scanning angle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why do we completely ignore pure Polar Coordinates in Physics III?**
> You will rarely (if ever) solve a real electromagnetism problem using only two-dimensional polar coordinates. In Physics III, real-world problems have volume or generate three-dimensional effects in space.
> 
> The **Cylindrical** system is the true sovereign of exams. When you reach Gauss's Law, you will see that cylindrical Gaussian surfaces are used to solve wires, coaxial cables, and infinite flat plates (where the base of the cylinder lies on the $xy$ plane and the height follows the field lines). Mastering the cylindrical kit now is what will save you from crying in the next chapter.

#### 🧰 The Complete Cylindrical Conversion Kit

To transition freely between both worlds while setting up integrals, you must keep the forward and backward equations of the system in mind:

**1. Translating from Cylindrical to Cartesian (Forward):**
Used to decompose radial vectors into the fixed laboratory directions ($\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$).

$$
x = \rho \cos(\phi)
$$

$$
y = \rho \sin(\phi)
$$

$$
z = z
$$

> [!TIP]
> I like to imagine the following scenario to understand this:
>
> I perceive the world in Cartesian coordinates, so we have the classic $(x, y, z)$ axes. To convert them into this new coordinate system, just use the $xy$ plane as the base of your cylinder. Since the base is a circle, I need a radius ($\rho$) rotating 360° on this plane to form it. If you draw this radius, you will notice that you can decompose it onto the x or y axis in several ways. You can view this as a sum of position vectors or use trigonometry to reach the relationship above—just do it.
>
> Now we have our base, we just need our flagpole. So far, this process has converted Cartesian $\to$ Polar. Now we need to convert that into Cylindrical. Let's face it, a circle with height is what we call a cylinder, which is why our z-axis serves as the flagpole. Notice that it does not change from Cartesian $\to$ Cylindrical.

**2. Translating from Cartesian to Cylindrical (Backward):**
Used to simplify polynomial denominators in the integral via the Pythagorean Theorem on the plane.

$$
\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}
$$

$$
\phi = \arctan\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)
$$

$$
z = z
$$

#### Mapping the Position Vectors in Our Scenario:

1. **Vector where the charge is located ($\vec{r}'$):** The charge element is located directly on the wire (the central flagpole $z$). Therefore, its radial distance to the center is zero ($\rho = 0$). This vector only has height: $\vec{r}' = z'\hat{k}$.
2. **Vector where the target point $P$ is located ($\vec{r}$):** Using radar mode, we say that point $P$ is at a radial distance $\rho$ from the flagpole, pointing outward in a unit direction that we will call $\hat{a}_\rho$: $\vec{r} = \rho\hat{a}_\rho + z\hat{k}$.

> 🚨 **The Radial Unit Vector ($\hat{a}_\rho$):** Unlike fixed Cartesian vectors ($\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$), the unit vector $\hat{a}_\rho$ is dynamic and depends strictly on the rotation angle $\phi$ of the radar:

$$
\hat{a}_\rho = \cos(\phi)\hat{i} + \sin(\phi)\hat{j}
$$

> Since the circle features a complete sweep of $2\pi$ (360°), the computer or engineer chooses where they want to look:
> * If we strategically position point $P$ on the x-axis ($\phi = 0^\circ$), we get $\hat{a}_\rho = (1, 0, 0) = \hat{i}$.
> * If we position it on the y-axis ($\phi = 90^\circ$), we get $\hat{a}_\rho = (0, 1, 0) = \hat{j}$.
>
> It acts as an infinity of available unit vectors to define which perpendicular direction the field is pushing.

> [!TIP]
> Notice that it is called a *radial unit vector*, meaning that regardless of the angle you select, its magnitude will always be 1. Test it out and draw your own conclusions.

Subtracting the vectors to obtain the **Relative Position Vector ($\vec{r}_{\text{rel}}$)**:

$$
\vec{r}_{\text{rel}} = \vec{r} - \vec{r}' = \rho\hat{a}_\rho + (z - z')\hat{k}
$$

> [!NOTE]
> Here you only deal with the radius and the z-axis, which is much easier than dealing with 3 separate axes.

#### The Intensity Modulus (The Denominator):
Applying the backward equation from our kit ($\rho^2 = x^2+y^2$), the magnitude of the vector in cylindrical space swallows the horizontal Cartesian components via Pythagoras:

$$
|\vec{r}_{\text{rel}}| = \sqrt{\rho^2 + (z - z')^2}
$$

Substituting the translation of $dq = \lambda dz'$ and the cylindrical geometry into our integral, it assumes this structured form:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int \frac{\lambda dz'}{[\rho^2 + (z - z')^2]^{3/2}} \left[ \rho\hat{a}_\rho + (z - z')\hat{k} \right]
$$

---

### Step 3: The Vector Symmetry Filter

If we expand the integral, we will have two separate components: one pushing radially away from the wire ($\hat{a}_\rho$) and another pushing vertically up or down along the flagpole ($\hat{k}$).

Analyzing the vertical component associated with the term $(z - z')$: if the wire is considered infinite or if our analysis point is located exactly at the center (mediatrix) of a finite wire, this function behaves as an **Odd Function** under symmetric integration limits.

By the properties of integral calculus, integrating an odd function over mirrored intervals results strictly in zero ($\int dE_z = 0$). Physically, this means that for every piece of charge $dz'$ on the top part of the flagpole pushing point $P$ downward, there will be a twin piece on the bottom part pushing it upward with the exact same force. The vertical components mutually cancel out.



The flagpole component vanishes, leaving exclusively the pure radial projection perpendicular to the wire:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{\lambda \rho \hat{a}_\rho}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int \frac{dz'}{[\rho^2 + (z - z')^2]^{3/2}}
$$

---

### Step 4: The Calculus Engine (Trigonometric Substitution)

To solve this algebraic monster with a fractional power without relying on pre-made calculus tables, we use a geometric "hack". Instead of tracking charges by measuring the wire in meters ($dz'$), we track them by measuring the inclination of our line of sight via the angle $\theta$.



#### 1. The Angular Translation Mechanism:
Looking at the right triangle formed by the fixed distance $\rho$ and the variable height $(z - z')$, we apply the tangent function:

$$
\tan(\theta) = \frac{z - z'}{\rho} \implies (z - z') = \rho\tan(\theta)
$$

Derivating the equation with respect to the angle, we convert the spatial differential into an angular differential (remembering that the derivative of tangent is secant squared):

$$
dz' = \rho\sec^2(\theta)d\theta
$$

To clean up the denominator, we apply the fundamental trigonometric identity $1 + \tan^2(\theta) = \sec^2(\theta)$:

$$
\left[ \rho^2 + (\rho\tan(\theta))^2 \right]^{3/2} = \left[ \rho^2(1 + \tan^2(\theta)) \right]^{3/2} = \left[ \rho^2\sec^2(\theta) \right]^{3/2} = \rho^3\sec^3(\theta)
$$

#### 2. Cancellation and Simplification:
When substituting these translations back into the main integral, the complex functions undergo a drastic simplification:

$$
\int \frac{\rho\sec^2(\theta)d\theta}{\rho^3\sec^3(\theta)} \implies \frac{1}{\rho^2} \int \frac{1}{\sec(\theta)}d\theta \implies \frac{1}{\rho^2} \int \cos(\theta)d\theta
$$

The secret of this method was transforming a polynomial sum with a fractional power into a direct cosine integral.

## 🏁 The Final Leap: Boundary Contours

Since the integral of cosine is sine ($\int \cos\theta d\theta = \sin\theta$), we simply apply the geometric boundaries of the analyzed physical body.

### Scenario A: The Infinite Wire ($\infty$)
For a wire that extends infinitely in both directions of the flagpole, the viewing angle of our "radar" needs to scan the entire horizon, extending from $-90^\circ$ to $+90^\circ$ (or from $-\pi/2$ to $+\pi/2$ radians):

$$
\int_{-\pi/2}^{+\pi/2} \cos(\theta)d\theta = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) - \sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 1 - (-1) = 2
$$

Combining the numerical result with the constant we isolated outside the integral block in **Step 3**:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{\lambda \rho \hat{a}_\rho}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \cdot \left( \frac{2}{\rho^2} \right) \implies \vec{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0\rho}\hat{a}_\rho
$$

---

### Scenario B: The Finite Wire
If the bar has a real delimited length from $L_1$ to $L_2$, our radar will not be able to open all the way to $90^\circ$. The integral will take the limits of the real physical angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ that point to the extremities of the bar:

$$
\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \cos(\theta)d\theta = \left[ \sin(\theta_2) - \sin(\theta_1) \right]
$$

> ⚠️ **Caution Regarding Symmetry Breaking:** If point $P$ is shifted closer to one of the ends of the finite wire (off the central mediatrix), the Symmetry Filter from **Step 3** fails. Because the top and bottom charges do not perfectly balance each other out, the vertical component $\hat{k}$ does not yield zero. The resulting electric field will exert an oblique diagonal force, pushing the point away in the radial direction ($\hat{a}_\rho$) and simultaneously pulling/pushing it laterally along the flagpole direction ($\hat{k}$).

To avoid calculating abstract angles in degrees during exams, engineers extract the sine value directly from the geometry of the right triangle ($\sin(\theta) = \frac{\text{Opposite Side}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}$):

$$
\sin(\theta) = \frac{z - z'}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + (z - z')^2}}
$$

Substituting these limits for a linear rod that extends symmetrically from $-L/2$ to $+L/2$, the radial electric field assumes the following consolidated engineering equation:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0\rho} \left[ \frac{L_2}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + L_2^2}} - \frac{L_1}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + L_1^2}} \right] \hat{a}_\rho
$$
