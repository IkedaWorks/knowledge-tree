
# 📐 Theory: Volumetric Distributions — Generic Derivation of a Charged Sphere

The calculation of the electric field for three-dimensional bodies expands our infinitesimal modeling from a two-dimensional surface to a true volume. By utilizing radial symmetry and the spherical coordinate system, we can map the charge scattered across a solid body with surgical precision. This document outlines the general derivation for a uniformly charged solid sphere, the definitive foundation for electrostatic shielding and advanced applications of Gauss's Law.

---

## 🌎 Analysis Scenario and Axis Selection

Imagine a solid, non-conducting sphere of radius $R$ carrying a uniform total volumetric charge $Q$. To extract maximum efficiency from nature's native symmetries, we place the center of the sphere perfectly at the origin of our coordinate system $(0, 0, 0)$. Our goal is to map the electric field $\vec{E}$ at any point in space located at a radial distance $r$ from the center.



> 💡 **Engineering Insight:** Why lock the center of the sphere to the origin and abandon the cylindrical/Cartesian systems? A sphere features perfect radial symmetry. If we tried to slice this body using tiny Cartesian cubes ($dx \cdot dy \cdot dz$), the integration limits would yield intractable three-dimensional polynomial equations. By using the center as our global origin, we collapse the three-dimensional sweep into an analysis that depends strictly on a single variable: the linear distance to the center.

---

## 🛠️ The Resolution Pipeline (4-Step Algorithm)

### Step 1: Transitioning from the Surface Model to the Volumetric Model (The Physics)

When dealing with bodies that possess actual physical volume, we monitor the charge distribution per unit of three-dimensional space. We define the Volumetric Charge Density, represented here by $\rho$.

$$
\rho = \frac{Q}{V}
$$

S.I. Unit of measurement: $\text{Coulomb}/\text{meter}^3$.

> [!NOTE]
> To avoid mental confusion in our engineering variable soup, we use the letter $r'$ to represent the radius of our infinitesimal spherical shells, leaving the letter $r$ free for the position of our target analysis point.

For a continuous, uniform distribution, this ratio scales down perfectly to an infinitesimal patch of volume $dV$:

$$
dq = \rho \cdot dV
$$

To scan the sphere without drowning in raw triple integrals, we apply the concept of concentric accumulation. We imagine the solid sphere as a three-dimensional onion composed of infinite spherical shells of radius $r'$ and infinitesimal thickness $dr'$.



The surface area of one of these spherical shells is given by $4\pi (r')^2$. Multiplying this surface area by its infinitesimal thickness $dr'$ yields our differential volume element:

$$
dV = 4\pi (r')^2 dr'
$$

Substituting this geometric identity into our charge equation gives our final physics translation:

$$
dq = \rho \cdot 4\pi (r')^2 dr'
$$

---

### Step 2: The Conversion Kit (Transitioning to Spherical Coordinates)

Instead of deriving relative distance vectors through heavy oblique trigonometry in three dimensions, we rely on the Spherical Coordinate System. In it, any point in space is mapped by a direct distance to the origin and two scanning angles. Since the electric field generated by a spherical distribution points strictly outward or inward from the center, we define the Radial Spherical Unit Vector $\hat{a}_r$.

Mapping the geometric components in our scenario:

1. **Vector where the charge element is located:** $\vec{r}' = r'\hat{a}_r$
2. **Vector where the target point P is located:** $\vec{r} = r\hat{a}_r$

Subtracting the vectors to obtain the Relative Position Vector:

$$
\vec{r}_{\text{rel}} = \vec{r} - \vec{r}' = (r - r')\hat{a}_r
$$

#### The Intensity Modulus (The Denominator):
Since both vectors are aligned along the exact same radial line in spherical space, the magnitude of the relative distance is simply the direct scalar difference between the radii:

$$
|\vec{r}_{\text{rel}}| = |r - r'|
$$

Assembling these components into the continuous infinitesimal form of Coulomb's Law gives our structured setup equation:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_{0}^{R} \frac{\rho \cdot 4\pi (r')^2 dr'}{|r - r'|^2} \hat{a}_r
$$

---

### Step 3: The Vector Symmetry Filter

If we were to expand this integral in native Cartesian coordinates, we would have to calculate separate components for $\hat{i}$, $\hat{j}$, and $\hat{k}$. 

However, the Symmetry Filter acts with devastating efficiency on spherical bodies. For every single charge point located on the upper or left half of the sphere, there is a twin point located exactly on the opposite side ($180^\circ$ angular offset).



The transverse and lateral components of these forces mutually cancel out across all three-dimensional space. The resulting electric field can only possess a single surviving component: the pure radial component. All the vector complexity of the problem collapses into a purely scalar integration along the radius:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{\rho \cdot \hat{a}_r}{\epsilon_0} \int \frac{(r')^2 dr'}{|r - r'|^2}
$$

---

### Step 4: The Calculus Engine (The Boundary Bifurcation)

Unlike line and surface integrals, the modulus in the denominator behaves in completely distinct ways depending on where we position our observation point $P$. This breaks our resolution into two independent calculus tracks.

#### Case 1: The Target Point P is Outside the Sphere ($r \ge R$)
When viewing the object from the outside, the observation radius $r$ is always larger than the radius of any internal shell ($r > r'$). Thus, the modulus simplifies directly. Solving the accumulation integral for all shells from $0$ to the physical limit of the sphere $R$:

$$
\int_{0}^{R} \frac{(r')^2 dr'}{r^2} = \frac{R^3}{3r^2}
$$

Substituting this result back into the Step 3 equation:

$$
\vec{E}_{\text{external}} = \frac{\rho \cdot R^3}{3\epsilon_0 r^2} \hat{a}_r
$$

#### Case 2: The Target Point P is Inside the Sphere ($r < R$)
If we drive our radar inside the solid sphere, the shells located beyond our current position ($r' > r$) exert no net force on point $P$ due to the natural cancellation properties of spherical shells. Therefore, the calculus engine only accumulates charges from $0$ up to our current position $r$:

$$
\int_{0}^{r} \frac{(r')^2 dr'}{r^2} = \frac{r}{3}
$$

Substituting this back into the Step 3 equation:

$$
\vec{E}_{\text{internal}} = \frac{\rho \cdot r}{3\epsilon_0} \hat{a}_r
$$

---

## 🏁 The Final Leap: Boundary Contours

Combining both solutions in terms of the total charge of the sphere, we can substitute the macroscopic definition of volumetric density to obtain our consolidated engineering equations.

### Scenario A: Exterior Region ($r \ge R$)
Substituting the density into the Case 1 equation, the radius of the body $R^3$ cancels out, revealing that to the outside world, the sphere behaves exactly like a point charge concentrated at the origin:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} \hat{a}_r
$$

### Scenario B: Interior Region ($r < R$)
Substituting the density into the Case 2 equation, we discover that the electric field inside an insulating volumetric distribution grows perfectly **linearly** starting from the center, reaching its peak exactly at the surface of the body:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{Q \cdot r}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3} \hat{a}_r
$$
