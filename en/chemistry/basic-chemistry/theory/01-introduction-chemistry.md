## The Scope of Chemistry

Chemistry is the science dedicated to the study of the composition, structure, properties, and transformations of matter.

This definition presents four conceptual pillars that guide all chemical investigation:

* **Composition:** What matter is made of.
* **Structure:** How its components are arranged in space.
* **Properties:** How matter behaves and what characteristics it displays.
* **Transformations:** How its composition or structure can be altered.

When an iron bar rusts, when an effervescent tablet dissolves in water, or when a fuel burns, different phenomena occur. Chemistry seeks to explain these occurrences through models and principles that connect observable behavior to the hidden structure of matter.

## The Importance of Studying Chemistry

Matter constitutes the entirety of the physical world around us. The atmospheric air, water, food, medicines, fuels, metals, and semiconductors in electronic devices all have a composition and structure that directly determine their behavior.

Chemistry allows us to understand these relationships:

* Instead of merely recording that a substance possesses a certain characteristic, we investigate the structural cause behind that characteristic.
* Instead of merely observing that a reaction yields new products, we analyze how the initial components were rearranged.

This shift in perspective is the foundation of chemical thinking.

## The Concept of Matter

The primary subject of study in Chemistry is matter, traditionally defined as anything that has mass and occupies space.

A rock, an aliquot of water, the air inside a container, and the human body are all examples of matter. However, this general definition alone does not explain the behavior of materials.

Two samples can have identical masses yet display completely different behaviors. Under the same conditions of temperature and pressure, one substance may exist as a solid, while another exists as a gas. Therefore, Chemistry investigates not only how much matter exists, but also what it is made of and how its components are arranged.

## The Discontinuous Nature of Matter

Macroscopically, matter appears to be a continuous medium. When observing a glass of water, individual units are not distinguished; rather, it appears as a uniform fluid mass.

Chemistry uses a submicroscopic description in which matter is discontinuous, consisting of fundamental entities such as atoms, molecules, and ions. Although these entities are not directly visible in everyday life, their behaviors are measured and modeled scientifically.

### Macroscopic Scale

The macroscopic scale encompasses properties and phenomena that can be directly observed and measured:

* The color of a substance.
* The volume occupied by a liquid.
* The mass of a solid.
* Changes in states of matter.
* The formation of new substances in a reaction.

### Submicroscopic Scale

The submicroscopic scale explains macroscopic observations in terms of atomic and molecular structure. Instead of merely recording the evaporation of water, we analyze the dynamics of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules during the phase transition. In a chemical reaction, we investigate how the atoms of the reactants rearrange to form the products.
## Fundamental Entities: Atoms, Elements, Molecules, and Ions

The submicroscopic description uses specific concepts to represent matter:

* **Atom:** The fundamental unit of matter, consisting of a dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by an electron cloud occupied by electrons.
* **Chemical Element:** A set of atoms that have the same number of protons in their nucleus. This value is called the **atomic number ($Z$)**:

$$Z = N_p$$

Where $N_p$ represents the number of protons. An atom containing $1$ proton belongs to the element hydrogen, while an atom with $8$ protons belongs to the element oxygen. The number of protons defines the identity of the element.

Atoms interact to form more complex arrangements:

* **Molecule:** An electrically neutral entity formed by atoms bonded together by chemical bonds. Water, for example, is represented by the chemical formula $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, indicating that each molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
* **Ions:** Chemical species that possess a net electric charge due to an imbalance between the number of protons and electrons. The loss of electrons produces a **cation** (positive charge), while the gain of electrons produces an **anion** (negative charge).

## Relationship between Composition, Structure, and Properties

The composition and structure of a material system determine its properties. Two substances can contain the exact same chemical elements, yet the way their atoms are arranged and bonded can produce entirely different behaviors.

This central connection is expressed by the principle:

$$\text{composition} + \text{structure} \longrightarrow \text{properties}$$

Knowing which elements make up a substance is only the first step; understanding its behavior requires analyzing how these elements are arranged and how they interact with one another.

## Transformations of Matter

Matter can undergo changes classified according to whether its chemical identity is maintained or altered:

* **Physical Transformations:** Do not alter the chemical composition of the substance. The transition of liquid water to a gaseous state changes its state of aggregation while maintaining the composition $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
* **Chemical Transformations (Reactions):** Alter the chemical identity of the initial substances. The atoms of the reactants are rearranged to form products with new properties:

$$\text{reactants} \longrightarrow \text{products}$$

## Conservation of Mass

In ordinary chemical reactions, the initial substances are transformed, but the atoms involved are conserved. The bonds between the atoms present in the reactants are rearranged to form the products.

Consider the formation reaction of water:

$$2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

The total quantity of atoms of the elements hydrogen ($4$ atoms) and oxygen ($2$ atoms) remains identical before and after the reaction. This behavior grounds the Principle of Conservation of Mass in closed systems: during a chemical reaction, the total mass of the system remains constant.

## Building Chemical Reasoning

Learning Chemistry involves developing the ability to transition between different levels of abstraction. When faced with a visual phenomenon, such as a color change in a solution, chemical analysis follows this sequence:

$$\text{observe} \longrightarrow \text{represent} \longrightarrow \text{explain} \longrightarrow \text{predict}$$

1. **Observe:** Record the occurrence of the phenomenon on the macroscopic scale.
2. **Represent:** Use symbols, formulas, and equations to translate the event.
3. **Explain:** Propose a model on the submicroscopic scale to ground the result.
4. **Predict:** Apply the constructed model to anticipate the system's behavior under new conditions.

## Scientific Models

A scientific model is a simplified representation used to describe, explain, or predict aspects of the natural world. Models are not exact replicas of reality, but rather conceptual tools.

Representing a molecule as rigid spheres connected by rods allows us to visualize atomic geometry and connectivity, even though atoms are not solid spheres. Studying Chemistry involves selecting the appropriate model for each level of analysis and recognizing its limits of application.

## The Quantitative Nature of Chemistry

Chemical analysis combines qualitative observations and quantitative measurements. Quantities such as mass, volume, temperature, pressure, and amount of substance can be expressed through mathematical relationships.

Density ($\rho$), for example, relates mass ($m$) to the volume ($V$) occupied by a body:

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

Measurable quantities and their applications will be detailed throughout the study of the properties of matter.

## Module Progression Map

The concepts introduced in this chapter establish the structural foundation for the following topics:

* Matter has **composition** and **structure**.
* Composition and structure determine **properties**.
* Matter undergoes **physical and chemical transformations**.
* Transformations are explained through **submicroscopic models** (atoms, elements, molecules, and ions).
* In chemical reactions, atoms are **rearranged and conserved**.

These principles guide future in-depth studies on atomic structure, chemical bonding, the periodic table, and stoichiometry.
