# Properties and States of Matter

> Rigorous characterization of matter requires differentiating between its measurable dimensions, analyzing interparticle forces across various aggregation states, and understanding transformations that either preserve or alter the identity of substances.

---

## Classification of the Properties of Matter

Properties of matter are measurable or observable attributes that describe the behavior of a physicochemical system under defined thermodynamic conditions. Rigorous characterization of a material demands distinguishing between universal and specific attributes, as well as understanding the scale dependence of these quantities relative to the amount of matter present in the system.

---

### General and Specific Properties

#### General Properties
Intrinsic attributes common to any portion of matter, regardless of its chemical composition or atomic structure. Because they are universal, they do not provide analytical parameters for identifying or differentiating pure substances.

* **Mass ($m$)**: A quantitative measure of inertia and the amount of matter contained within a system.
  * *Example*: $1\text{ kg}$ of lead and $1\text{ kg}$ of cotton possess strictly the same mass, despite occupying vastly different volumes.
* **Volume ($V$)**: The extent of three-dimensional space occupied by a portion of matter.
  * *Example*: $1\text{ L}$ of water completely fills the interior of a cubic container measuring $10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm}$.
* **Impenetrability**: A physical principle grounded in interatomic electrostatic repulsive forces, stating that two bodies cannot occupy the exact same space simultaneously.
  * *Example*: The water level in a graduated cylinder rises by a volume exactly equal to that of a submerged metallic sphere.
* **Inertia**: The tendency of a body to resist changes in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line.
  * *Example*: The force required to accelerate a $10\text{ kg}$ iron block is significantly greater than that required to accelerate a $1\text{ kg}$ block.

#### Specific Properties
Physicochemical constants and intrinsic behaviors that depend directly on the chemical identity and structural arrangement of the substance, allowing for its unambiguous identification.

* **Physical Properties**: Quantities measurable without altering chemical composition or breaking intramolecular bonds.
  * *Example*: The density ($\rho$) of pure water at $25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and $1\text{ atm}$ is $0.997\text{ g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}$, whereas that of mercury under the same conditions is $13.53\text{ g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}$.
* **Chemical Properties**: The transformative capacity of a substance upon reacting with other reagents or under the influence of energy, altering its atomic structure.
  * *Example*: Iron undergoes oxidation in the presence of oxygen and moisture to form iron(III) hydroxide (rust), whereas gold remains inert under identical conditions.
* **Organoleptic Properties**: Attributes detectable by human sensory receptors, used exclusively in non-hazardous preliminary screening.
  * *Example*: The sharp, pungent odor characteristic of acetic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) compared to the mild odor of ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$).

### Intensive and Extensive Properties

In formal thermodynamic analysis, property classification is strictly based on whether the measured value depends on the scale, mass, or number of particles ($n$) contained within the system.

#### Extensive Properties
Quantities whose numerical values are directly proportional to the total mass or mole number of the system. If a homogeneous system is divided into smaller fractions, the value of an extensive property decreases in the exact same proportion.

* **Mass ($m$)**: Depends directly on the amount of matter.
  * *Example*: Removing half of the liquid from a flask containing $500\text{ g}$ of ethanol reduces the remaining mass of the system to $250\text{ g}$.
* **Volume ($V$)**: Depends on the space occupied by the amount of matter.
  * *Example*: $2\text{ moles}$ of an ideal gas at STP occupy $44.8\text{ L}$, whereas $1\text{ mole}$ occupies exactly $22.4\text{ L}$.
* **Heat Capacity ($C$)**: The amount of thermal energy required to raise the temperature of the entire system by $1\text{ K}$ (or $1\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$).
  * *Example*: An Olympic-sized swimming pool full of water requires an immensely larger total amount of heat to rise $1\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ than a cup containing $200\text{ mL}$ of the same water.
* **Internal Energy ($U$)**: The sum of all kinetic and potential energies of the system's constituent particles.
  * *Example*: $100\text{ g}$ of water at $50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ stores twice the internal energy of $50\text{ g}$ of water at $50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

#### Intensive Properties
Quantities independent of the amount of matter, total mass, or sample size. They remain invariant upon subdividing the system and depend solely on the nature of the substance and the thermodynamic state (such as pressure and temperature) in which it exists.

* **Temperature ($T$)**: A measure of the average kinetic energy per particle.
  * *Example*: If a pitcher with $1\text{ L}$ of water at $80\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ is poured into four $250\text{ mL}$ glasses, the temperature of the water in each glass remains $80\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
* **Density ($\rho$)**: A constant ratio between two extensive quantities (mass and volume).
  * *Example*: $1\text{ drop}$ of pure water and $1,000\text{ liters}$ of pure water share the exact same density ($\rho = 1.00\text{ g/cm}^3$ at $4\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), whether on Earth or in space, because the mass per unit volume remains identical.
* **Specific Heat Capacity ($c$)**: The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of exactly $1\text{ g}$ of a substance by $1\text{ K}$.
  * *Example*: The specific heat capacity of water is always $4.184\text{ J}\cdot\text{g}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$, regardless of whether analyzing a single raindrop or an entire ocean.
* **Melting Point ($T_m$) and Boiling Point ($T_b$)**: Temperatures at which phase transitions occur under a specified external pressure.
  * *Example*: Pure water boils at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ under $1\text{ atm}$ of pressure, whether boiling $10\text{ mL}$ in a test tube or $10\text{ L}$ in an industrial boiler.

> **Formal Note**: The mathematical ratio between two extensive properties invariably yields an intensive property. The density of a homogeneous phase is defined as:
> 
> $$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$
> 
> Since mass ($m$) and volume ($V$) scale in identical proportions, the quotient $\rho$ remains constant, acting as an intensive signature of the material.

## Aggregation States of Matter

Matter presents itself in different physical states (or phases) determined by the competitive balance between two submicroscopic energies:

* **Interparticle Attraction Energy ($E_{\text{att}}$)**: Cohesive intermolecular or ionic forces tending to draw particles together and order them.
* **Thermal Kinetic Energy ($E_{\text{kin}}$)**: Energy associated with particle movement and agitation, directly proportional to the absolute temperature ($T$) of the system.

### The Three Fundamental States

* **Solid State**: Attraction energy heavily dominates over kinetic energy ($E_{\text{att}} \gg E_{\text{kin}}$). Particles occupy fixed spatial positions, displaying only vibrational movement around equilibrium points.
  * *Macroscopic Behavior*: Fixed shape and volume, high incompressibility.
* **Liquid State**: Attraction energy and kinetic energy are of comparable magnitude ($E_{\text{att}} \approx E_{\text{kin}}$). Cohesive forces suffice to keep particles aggregated, but not to lock them into rigid positions, enabling fluid sliding and translational motion.
  * *Macroscopic Behavior*: Definite volume, but variable shape (conforms to container).
* **Gas State**: Kinetic energy significantly overcomes interparticle attractive forces ($E_{\text{kin}} \gg E_{\text{att}}$). Particles move randomly, rapidly, and independently in all directions, colliding elastically with each other and container walls.
  * *Macroscopic Behavior*: Variable shape and volume (high expandability and compressibility).

### Internal Structure of Solids: Crystalline versus Amorphous

Macroscopic rigidity in a solid does not guarantee perfect atomic ordering. Microscopically, solids fall into two fundamental classes:

* **Crystalline Solids**: Possess a highly ordered, three-dimensionally periodic atomic, ionic, or molecular arrangement known as a crystal lattice. Due to this structural uniformity, they feature sharp, well-defined melting points and property anisotropy.
* **Amorphous Solids**: Lack long-range spatial ordering. Structurally and thermodynamically, they are considered supercooled liquids with extremely high viscosity.
  * *Key Example*: Common silica glass, various synthetic polymers, and pitch. When heated, amorphous solids do not melt at a fixed temperature; instead, they soften gradually over a continuous temperature interval.

### Extreme States of Matter

Under conditions of temperature and pressure far removed from ambient environments, matter assumes specialized aggregation states:

* **Plasma**: Regarded as the fourth state of matter. Occurs when a gas is subjected to extremely high temperatures or intense electric fields, supplying energy sufficient to overcome the atomic ionization potential. The result is a macroscopically neutral yet highly conductive fluid composed of free electrons and positive ions.
* **Bose-Einstein Condensate**: Occurs when an ultra-low-density gas of bosons is cooled to temperatures extremely close to absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$). Under these conditions, the matter wave extent of the particles overlaps, causing a macroscopic fraction of atoms to occupy the same fundamental quantum state. The system behaves as a single macroscopic quantum entity.

## Physical and Chemical Phenomena

Transformations occurring in material systems are categorized based on whether the identity of the involved substances is preserved or altered.

### Physical Phenomena

Transformations in which the chemical composition of the system remains unchanged. Intermolecular interactions may be broken or formed, but intramolecular chemical bonds defining the substances are not modified.

* **Characteristics**: Often simple reversibility; absence of new substance formation.
* **Examples**: Physical state changes (such as water freezing), mechanical deformations, solid fragmentation, and molecular solute dissolution without reaction.

### Chemical Phenomena

Processes involving the redistribution of chemical bonds, resulting in reactant consumption and the formation of new substances with entirely distinct physical and chemical properties. A chemical transformation is formally represented by a balanced chemical equation:

$$\text{Reactants} \longrightarrow \text{Products}$$

* **Macroscopic Evidence Indicating a Chemical Reaction**:
  1. Color change not resulting from mere dilution.
  2. Effervescence or gas evolution without prior external heating.
  3. Formation of an insoluble solid precipitate upon mixing clear solutions.
  4. Pronounced thermal variation (heat absorption/release) or light emission.
* **Examples**: Hydrocarbon combustion, metal oxidation (corrosion), metabolic fermentation processes, and acid-base neutralization reactions.
