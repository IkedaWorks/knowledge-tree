# Percentage Definition

To truly understand what a percentage is, we must first recall the core concept of a **fraction**. A fraction is a way to express "how many parts in relation to a whole"—a different perspective on division.

In simpler terms: if you eat $1$ slice of a pizza that was divided into $8$ pieces, you now have $1$ piece less in relation to a whole of $8$ pizza slices. We represent this relation as $\frac{1}{8}$.

A fraction can have multiple interpretations—much like texts, poems, and art; things usually understood as belonging to the "humanities." In computing terms, we can say that fractions are **polymorphic**. While it can mean _"part of a whole"_, it can simultaneously mean _"how many times quantity $x$ fits inside $y$"_.

Using the same pizza analogy, we could interpret $\frac{1}{8}$ as: _into how many parts can I divide a pizza to satisfy $8$ people?_

Notice that mathematics does not stray far from the humanities in certain aspects. In cases like this, exactness blends seamlessly with subjectivity.

## Connecting Fractions to Percentages

A percentage is essentially a fraction that serves as a universal baseline to simplify problems requiring **a part in relation to a whole**. It streamlines calculations because it always locks the "whole" to the integer **$100$**.

This number represents the total amount of any given quantity, regardless of its actual value. It is the exact same logic behind standard units of measurement we use daily; a meter, for instance, was originally defined based on a physical platinum bar reference.

### How do we interpret a percentage?

Fundamentally, we have a part in relation to a whole locked at $100$:

$$\frac{x}{100} \quad \text{or} \quad x : 100$$

We represent this fraction using the notation: `numerator%`. This is a simplified, much more readable format. For example, instead of writing $\frac{5}{100}$, you simply write $5\%$.

## Practical Application

Whenever you encounter a scenario where you know a specific portion of a total quantity and want to determine its structural impact, you will use percentages.

### Modeling Examples:

- **The Cake Scenario:** You took a cake, cut it into $6$ slices, and ate $1$ slice. You have a portion relative to the original $6$ slices. This means you consumed $\frac{1}{6}$ of $100\%$. A clean way to model this mathematically is:
    
    $$\frac{1}{6} \cdot 100\% \approx 16.66\%$$
    
    In other words, the slice you ate is equivalent to approximately $16.66\%$ of the entire cake.
    
- **The Weight Scenario:** A doctor states that a $100\text{ kg}$ patient needs to lose $50\%$ of their current weight to enter a healthy range. This means they need to shed $\frac{50}{100}$ of $100\text{ kg}$. Recalling the concept of **equivalent fractions**, $\frac{50}{100}$ can be simplified to its irreducible form:
    
    $$\frac{50}{100} = \frac{1}{2}$$
    
    We divide both the numerator and the denominator by the same common factor until they can no longer be divided by whole numbers. Intuitively, you perceive that $50\%$ is exactly equivalent to $\frac{1}{2}$ (half) of their current weight.
    

>  **In other words:** A percentage is a baseline reference that you either multiply by or solve for to simplify part-to-whole relationships.
> 
> A percentage is a **direct proportion** where one of the terms is fixed ($100$). This allows us to compare quantities from entirely different dimensions as if they were placed on the exact same measuring scale.
